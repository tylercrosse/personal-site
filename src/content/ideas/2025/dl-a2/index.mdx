## Introduction

Assignment 2 shifts the course from derivation to controlled experimentation. The project begins with hand-built convolution and pooling layers, then moves to PyTorch models on CIFAR-10, and finally asks what happens when the data distribution is imbalanced and accuracy is no longer the whole story.

That makes the assignment a bridge between two kinds of understanding:

- why convolutional models work in the first place,
- and why experiment discipline matters once a framework lets you iterate quickly.

> [!note]
> This write-up keeps the assignment context at a high level and stays on the integrity-safe side of the course boundary.

## What the Project Made Explicit

At a high level, the assignment asked me to do three related things:

1. implement core CNN building blocks from scratch,
2. move to framework-scale training on CIFAR-10,
3. reason about imbalance and confidence, not just raw accuracy.

The underlying lesson is that computer vision performance comes from both architecture and protocol. Convolution gives the model the right bias for images, but the training loop, data transforms, and evaluation setup decide whether the comparison you are making is meaningful.

The repo ended up mirroring that split. There is one layer of work dedicated to implementing and checking the low-level operations, and another dedicated to running controlled PyTorch experiments, tracking trial configurations, and comparing models under fixed evaluation rules.

## Convolution Is a Structured Prior

The central idea behind CNNs is not "use a kernel." It is that the model should exploit the spatial structure already present in images. Convolution does that by enforcing two strong assumptions:

- nearby pixels matter together,
- and the same local feature can appear in many places.

<Figure
  src={p2Fig1}
  alt="Convolutional pipeline"
  caption="Convolution works because it respects locality and reuses detectors across spatial positions. Source: Lowyx GT DL Notes (CC BY 4.0), https://lowyx.com/posts/gt-dl-notes/."
  width={900}
/>

For a kernel of size `K`, stride `S`, and padding `P`, the spatial output size is

$$
H_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{H + 2P - K}{S} \right\rfloor + 1,
\qquad
W_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{W + 2P - K}{S} \right\rfloor + 1.
$$

That formula is easy to forget once frameworks hide it. It still matters, because many practical mistakes in CNN work are shape-policy mistakes: too much downsampling, mismatched feature-map sizes, or pooling decisions that throw away detail too early.

In the project, building convolution and max-pooling from scratch made those assumptions visible. The layers were no longer just boxes in a diagram; they were concrete operations with cost, geometry, and failure modes.

## Receptive Fields Explain Why Depth Helps

The next idea that becomes clearer in vision models is the receptive field: how much of the input can influence a unit at a given depth. Early layers see tiny neighborhoods. Deeper layers aggregate larger and larger context.

<Figure
  src={p2Fig2}
  alt="Receptive field growth"
  caption="Receptive field growth is the reason deeper layers can move from edges and textures toward shapes and larger structures. Source: Lowyx GT DL Notes (CC BY 4.0), https://lowyx.com/posts/gt-dl-notes/."
  width={900}
/>

That idea is conceptually simple, but it explains a lot of design tradeoffs:

- too little context and the model cannot distinguish globally similar classes,
- too much aggressive downsampling and fine detail disappears,
- more depth does not automatically help unless the receptive field is growing in a way that matches the scale of the task.

This is one reason CNN design feels more principled once you stop thinking of layers as arbitrary stacks and start thinking in terms of information retained, compressed, or discarded at each stage.

## Training Pipelines Matter Almost as Much as Architecture

PyTorch makes experimentation faster, but it also makes it easier to confuse speed with rigor. Once I moved from NumPy layers to framework-scale training, the central question became less "can I implement this model?" and more "am I making a fair comparison?"

<Figure
  src={p2Fig4}
  alt="Training curve diagnostics"
  caption="Training curves are often the fastest way to separate real gains from schedule mismatch, overfitting, or noisy variance. Source: Lowyx GT DL Notes (CC BY 4.0), https://lowyx.com/posts/gt-dl-notes/."
  width={900}
/>

Three ideas mattered most here:

- **normalization and optimization interact**,
- **schedules change what counts as a fair baseline**,
- **repeatable evaluation is more informative than a lucky run**.

That is what the CIFAR portion of the assignment taught. The framework removes derivative bookkeeping, but it does not remove the need for careful protocol design. If one model gets a different schedule, a more permissive augmentation policy, or a looser checkpoint rule, the comparison stops being informative.

**Illustrative toy snippet, not assignment code:**

```python
import torch

best_metric = float("-inf")
for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    model.train()
    for xb, yb in train_loader:
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = criterion(model(xb), yb)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()

    model.eval()
    with torch.no_grad():
        val_metric = evaluate(model, val_loader)

    if val_metric > best_metric:
        best_metric = val_metric
        best_state = {k: v.detach().clone() for k, v in model.state_dict().items()}
```

The habit that matters here is not the exact loop structure. It is separating training from evaluation and keeping the best validated state instead of assuming the latest state is the best one.

## Augmentation Is an Invariance Contract

Data augmentation is often described as a practical trick. A better framing is that augmentation states which transformations the model should treat as label-preserving.

<Figure
  src={p2Fig3}
  alt="Augmentation taxonomy"
  caption="Augmentation helps when the transformation matches an invariance you actually want the model to learn. Source: Lowyx GT DL Notes (CC BY 4.0), https://lowyx.com/posts/gt-dl-notes/."
  width={900}
/>

Random crops, flips, and color jitter are not inherently good. They help when they encode a sensible prior for the task, and they hurt when the transformation breaks the label semantics.

This was one of the broader lessons from the assignment: training data is not fixed just because the dataset is fixed. The transform pipeline is part of the model's worldview.

## Calibration and Imbalance Are First-Class Concerns

The last major idea from this assignment is that accuracy is too compressed to tell you what the model is doing. This becomes clear as soon as the class distribution becomes long-tailed or the output confidence is poorly matched to reality.

Expected calibration error (ECE) is one way to measure that mismatch:

$$
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_m|}{n}\left|\mathrm{acc}(B_m)-\mathrm{conf}(B_m)\right|.
$$

<Figure
  src={p2Fig5}
  alt="Calibration plot"
  caption="Calibration makes visible something top-1 accuracy cannot: whether the confidence values themselves are trustworthy. Source: Lowyx GT DL Notes (CC BY 4.0), https://lowyx.com/posts/gt-dl-notes/."
  width={900}
/>

The imbalanced-data section of the project made this concrete. Techniques like focal loss change which examples dominate the learning signal. Once classes are imbalanced, a model can look decent on average while under-serving precisely the cases you care about most.

**Illustrative toy snippet, not assignment code:**

```python
import torch

def expected_calibration_error(probs, labels, n_bins=10):
    conf = probs.max(dim=1).values
    pred = probs.argmax(dim=1)
    bins = torch.linspace(0, 1, n_bins + 1, device=probs.device)
    ece = torch.tensor(0.0, device=probs.device)

    for lo, hi in zip(bins[:-1], bins[1:]):
        mask = (conf >= lo) & (conf < hi)
        if mask.any():
            acc = (pred[mask] == labels[mask]).float().mean()
            ece += mask.float().mean() * (acc - conf[mask].mean()).abs()

    return ece
```

This kind of metric check matters because a model can improve on accuracy while still being overconfident or underconfident in ways that matter downstream.

## What This Assignment Made Concrete

By the end of the project, four ideas were much harder to forget:

- **Convolution is a design prior, not just a historical architecture choice.**
- **Framework speed increases the need for fair experimental controls.**
- **Augmentation changes the task the model is effectively being trained on.**
- **Accuracy alone is not enough once confidence and imbalance matter.**

That is also why the repo accumulated tuning scripts, saved trial configurations, and multiple checkpoints. The useful part of that machinery was not automation for its own sake; it was a way to make model comparisons reproducible enough to trust.

That combination makes this assignment a turning point in the course. It takes the mathematical foundations from the first project and pushes them into a setting where experimentation, not just derivation, becomes the main engineering skill.
