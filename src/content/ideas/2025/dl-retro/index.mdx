## Introduction

Graduate Deep Learning is organized as a sequence of model classes and training objectives. Each module introduces a new function family, a new optimization setup, and a new set of characteristic failure modes. By the end, the recurring question is not which architecture is best, but what assumptions each model family makes and which errors those assumptions tend to produce.

This post is not a memoir-style retrospective. It is a concept-first guide to the course: the ideas that each module tried to teach, why they matter, and which project in the series pushed that idea from theory into something operational.

> [!note]
> All of the companion posts in this series stay on the public, integrity-safe side of the line. I use my assignment work to motivate the concepts, but I do not publish graded solution details, code, or restricted handout content.

## Optimization Foundations

The course begins with linear models, explicit losses, and gradient descent rather than large architectures. That is where deep learning is easiest to reason about. If you cannot explain why a softmax classifier learns, why cross-entropy gradients have the form they do, or why a learning rate can destabilize a run, then larger models mainly add more ways to fail.

Three ideas from the early module ended up mattering everywhere else:

- **Scores are not probabilities.** A model needs both a predictive function and a loss that turns mistakes into gradient signal.
- **Backpropagation is graph bookkeeping.** The chain rule is the math, but the practical lesson is that every intermediate value and local derivative matters.
- **Optimization is geometry plus noise.** Batch size, scheduler shape, normalization, and regularization all change the surface you are traversing and the signal you see while traversing it.

That is also why the first project is a sensible starting point. Implementing a training loop without high-level framework abstractions makes it obvious that "the model" is only one piece of the system. Data preprocessing, initialization, loss design, regularization, and logging are all part of the same optimization story.

The repo work for that project made the same point operational: tests, numerical checks, sweep plots, and best-checkpoint selection were part of the workflow rather than cleanup after the fact.

The deeper dive for this section is [Project 1: Foundations from Scratch](/ideas/2025/dl-a1/).

## Convolutional Representation Learning

The second conceptual move in the course is that architecture should reflect structure in the data. Images are not just long vectors; they contain locality, translation, and repeated small-scale motifs. Convolutional networks work because they encode those assumptions directly.

The main concepts here are straightforward and recur throughout the course:

- **Local connectivity** says a unit does not need to look at the whole image to detect an edge or corner.
- **Parameter sharing** says the same detector should work in many spatial locations, not only one.
- **Receptive field growth** explains why deeper layers move from texture and contour information toward larger semantic structures.

What the vision project added was not just "CNNs work on CIFAR-10." It made two broader points concrete. First, training pipelines can dominate architecture differences if preprocessing, augmentation, schedules, or checkpoint criteria are not controlled. Second, accuracy alone is an incomplete summary. Once you care about confidence quality, class imbalance, or deployment thresholds, calibration and error distribution matter just as much as top-1 score.

That is the bridge from textbook CNNs to real model development: architecture gives you the bias; the experiment protocol tells you whether you are learning anything trustworthy.

In practice, that meant validating manual layer implementations first, then moving to controlled baseline comparisons, tuning studies, and calibration-focused evaluation once the pipeline moved into PyTorch.

The deeper dive for this section is [Project 2: ConvNets and Training Pipelines](/ideas/2025/dl-a2/).

## Sequence Modeling and Attention

Sequence modeling introduces a different kind of difficulty. In image classification, the output is often one label. In translation and related NLP tasks, the output is another sequence whose quality depends on local choice, long-range structure, and inference policy.

The course progression in this module is especially instructive:

1. **Recurrent models** make temporal dependence explicit, but they compress too much of the source sequence into a narrow channel.
2. **Attention** relaxes that bottleneck by turning retrieval over source-side states into part of each decoding step.
3. **Transformers** go further by treating attention, positional information, residual paths, and feed-forward blocks as the primary computation pattern.

The main conceptual shift is that alignment becomes dynamic instead of fixed. Once you see attention as differentiable retrieval rather than just another layer type, later developments make much more sense. Transformers are not "just attention"; they are a way of organizing computation so that many relationships can be modeled in parallel while training remains stable.

The assignment also makes an important systems point: decoding policy is part of model quality. Greedy decoding, beam search, and sampling can change observed behavior enough that model comparisons become noisy if inference is not held fixed.

The repo structure reinforced that lesson: saved configurations, comparison scripts, and direct translation inspection sat alongside the training code because sequence models are hard to judge from a single scalar metric alone.

The deeper dive for this section is [Project 3: Seq2Seq, Attention, and Transformers](/ideas/2025/dl-a3/).

## Generative Modeling

Generative modeling is where the course stops letting you pretend that one metric or one objective can summarize everything. VAE, GAN, and diffusion models all try to model data, but they do it under very different contracts.

- **VAEs** trade some perceptual sharpness for stable training and a structured latent space.
- **GANs** can produce sharp samples, but the optimization problem is an adversarial game with unstable equilibria.
- **Diffusion models** trade sampling speed for very robust denoising-based training dynamics.

This module also forces a broader definition of evaluation. Sample quality, diversity, controllability, and compute cost can move in different directions at the same time. A model can produce sharper samples while covering less of the distribution, or optimize cleanly while still missing the cases you care about.

That makes generative-model choice inherently contextual. You are not choosing a universal winner; you are choosing which objective and failure mode profile matches your constraints.

That engineering pattern showed up clearly in the repo: separate configs for beta sweeps, mode-collapse stress tests, noise-schedule experiments, and saved sample grids across training. The debugging loop changed with the model family.

The deeper dive for this section is [Project 4: VAE, GAN, and Diffusion](/ideas/2025/dl-a4/).

## Representation Analysis and Interpretability

The final conceptual turn in the course is that behavior is not the whole story. If a model becomes more capable after fine-tuning, the next question is what changed internally and how confident we should be in any explanation.

This module matters because it reframes interpretability as an evidence problem:

- Behavior-level changes tell you that something improved or regressed.
- Representation-level analysis tells you where internal differences might live.
- Robustness checks tell you whether the proposed explanation survives alternate prompts, slices, or measurement choices.

That is a more reliable default than treating a visualization or similarity score as a full explanation. Interpretability methods are usually most valuable when they help you narrow hypotheses, compare model states, and calibrate confidence, not when they promise one perfect mechanistic story.

The final project repo also reinforced that interpretability is a pipeline problem, not just an analysis notebook: data curation, decontamination, token-budget evaluation, and matched-condition activation collection all had to be defined before any feature comparison was worth interpreting.

The deeper dive for this section is [Project 5: Representation Comparison and Interpretability](/ideas/2025/dl-final-project/).

## What Lasted

The most durable lesson from the course is that deep learning is easier to reason about when you ask the same five questions repeatedly:

1. What structure is this model family assuming about the data?
2. What loss is it actually optimizing?
3. What does the optimizer see during training, and how noisy is that signal?
4. Which failure modes are characteristic of this setup?
5. What evaluation protocol is rich enough to detect those failures?

In practice, those questions cash out as three habits: validate the implementation before trusting a curve, keep comparison conditions fixed before claiming an improvement, and choose metrics rich enough to expose the failure modes you expect.

The same questions apply to small MNIST models, CNNs on CIFAR, translation systems, generative models, and mechanistic-interpretability work. They are also a better long-term memory of the course than any single benchmark result.

What still feels open is the gap between clean conceptual understanding and reliable empirical behavior. The course makes it clear that modern deep learning works because architecture, objective, data, optimization, and evaluation are all pushing on the same system at once. That makes the field powerful, but it also makes it easy to confuse a successful run with a complete explanation.
