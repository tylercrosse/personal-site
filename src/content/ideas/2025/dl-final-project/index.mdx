The final project centers on a different question: what changed inside the model when performance improved? Modern language models often get better through fine-tuning or test-time scaling before there is a clean explanation for what internal computation actually changed.

The project fine-tuned **Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct** on the public `s1k` reasoning traces, evaluated how its reasoning performance changed as more generated tokens were allowed, and then used a Crosscoder to compare activations between the base and fine-tuned models.

This post is more project-specific than the earlier assignment write-ups. The goal is still conceptual: to explain the scope and limits of representation comparison, and why interpretability needs stronger evidence standards than a single visualization or similarity score.

> [!note]
> This write-up discusses methodology and findings without publishing private course logistics or solution-like implementation detail.

## Project Scope

The project had three moving parts:

1. fine-tune a small reasoning-capable language model on `s1k`,
2. evaluate how accuracy changes as the model gets more test-time tokens,
3. compare internal activations before and after fine-tuning with a Crosscoder.

The project also required curating and decontaminating training data, evaluating the model across several token budgets instead of one fixed decode length, and collecting activations under matched prompt slices so that the comparison target stayed well defined.

The results:

| Dataset  | Model      | Scaling | Accuracy |
|----------|------------|---------|----------|
| gsm8k    | Pretrained | -0.0014 | 41.8     |
| gsm8k    | Fine-tuned | 0.0277  | 51.6     |
| boolq    | Pretrained | 0.0020  | 11.7     |
| boolq    | Fine-tuned | 0.0078  | 10.5     |
| math-500 | Pretrained | 0.0115  | 34.8     |
| math-500 | Fine-tuned | 0.0253  | 48.0     |

The fine-tuned model improved on the math-heavy benchmarks and showed stronger positive scaling with extra generation budget. At the layer studied, representation comparison showed no large internal rewrite. That mismatch framed the project.

## Why Better Behavior Is Not Yet an Explanation

Standard model evaluation is output-first. Benchmark accuracy shows whether behavior improved, but it does not identify the internal change responsible.

Interpretability asks a harder question: what internal change produced the behavioral change, and what evidence supports that claim?

Several things can happen at once:

- the model can use existing features differently,
- a small representational shift can matter more than a large visible one,
- or the relevant change may happen in a different layer, on a different prompt slice, or under a different measurement.

Interpretability work has to be explicit about what kind of claim it is making. A visualization is weaker evidence than a claim about the mechanism responsible for the behavior change.

## Representation Comparison Starts with Matched Conditions

If you want to compare two models, you need to compare them under matched inputs and matched measurement choices. Otherwise, you risk attributing differences to the model when they actually come from prompt selection, tokenization differences, or noisy sampling.

The comparison setup was simple:

- take a base model and a fine-tuned model,
- run both on the same prompt set,
- capture activations at the same layer,
- and compare those activations in a shared feature space.

It breaks the problem into a more concrete decomposition:

- which features appear shared,
- which features appear specialized,
- and how stable those observations are across slices.

The hard part is that similarity does not imply causal importance. A feature can look different without explaining the behavioral gain, and an important causal shift can be too subtle for a coarse summary metric.

**Illustrative pseudocode, not project code:**

```text
prompts = sample_prompt_slice()

base_acts = collect_activations(base_model, prompts, layer=L)
tuned_acts = collect_activations(tuned_model, prompts, layer=L)

shared_space = fit_shared_dictionary(base_acts, tuned_acts)
shared_features, shifted_features = compare_feature_usage(
    shared_space, base_acts, tuned_acts
)

report(shared_features, shifted_features)
```

This is the minimum structure for an honest comparison: same prompts, same layer, same measurement choice, then a shared representation space for the actual comparison.

## Crosscoders Are Paired Sparse Autoencoders

The Crosscoder is the central modeling tool in this project: a sparse autoencoder designed to work on activations from two related models at once.

It learns a shared latent dictionary that tries to reconstruct both model states. That makes it useful for asking whether a feature is mostly shared, mostly model-specific, or differently weighted across the two models.

The training objective balances reconstruction quality and sparsity:

$$
\min_{W,B} \; \|X - W\,\sigma(B^\top X)\|_2^2 + \|Y - W\,\sigma(B^\top Y)\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\sigma(B^\top X)\|_1,
$$

where `X` and `Y` are activation sets from the base and fine-tuned models, and `\sigma` is ReLU.

In this objective:

- the reconstruction terms force the latent features to preserve information relevant to reconstruction,
- the sparsity term encourages a smaller, more interpretable active set,
- and the paired setup makes comparison between model states easier than two independent SAEs would.

Crosscoders provide a more structured comparison space. The scientific work still comes from deciding what counts as stable evidence inside that space.

## Behavior Metrics and Representation Metrics Answer Different Questions

Behavior-level metrics and representation-level metrics answer different questions and need to be read together.

Behavior-level evaluation answers:

- did the model get better on the task,
- and did more test-time computation help?

Representation-level analysis answers:

- where do the two models still look similar,
- where do they diverge,
- and are those divergences stable across prompts or slices?

The project used "scaling" as one of the behavior metrics: how much accuracy improved as the model was allowed more generated tokens. That metric is particularly relevant for reasoning work because it captures something closer to "benefit from additional thinking time" than one fixed-budget accuracy score can.

Behavior metrics do not identify internal features directly. Similarity statistics do not establish causality by themselves. Keeping that separation clear matters.

**Illustrative pseudocode, not project code:**

```text
dataset = curate_and_decontaminate(raw_sources)
tuned_model = fine_tune(base_model, dataset)

for budget in token_budgets:
    behavior_metrics[budget] = evaluate(tuned_model, tasks, max_tokens=budget)

matched_acts = collect_matched_activations(
    base_model, tuned_model, prompt_slice
)
representation_metrics = compare_internal_features(matched_acts)

combine(behavior_metrics, representation_metrics, robustness_checks)
```

The sequence matters. Behavior is evaluated across token budgets, representation is compared under matched conditions, and the final claim only becomes stronger if both kinds of evidence point in the same direction.

## Findings

The fine-tuned model improved on the math-focused tasks. At the layer analyzed, the Crosscoder found that most of the geometry remained shared.

A few plausible interpretations fit:

- the important change may be subtle in magnitude,
- the relevant shift may happen in later layers or under different prompts,
- or the fine-tuned model may be reusing broadly similar features with different coordination or weighting.

This kind of outcome is common in interpretability work. The analysis points toward a more conservative lesson: improved behavior can arise from changes that are distributed, conditional, and hard to isolate with one analysis method.

## Evidence Quality Matters More Than Pretty Visuals

Interpretability work is especially vulnerable to overconfident storytelling. It is easy to produce a compelling figure, a cluster plot, or a similarity score and then say more than the evidence warrants.

Interpretability claims usually need three layers:

1. **behavior evidence**: did something measurable change?
2. **representation evidence**: do internal activations show structured differences?
3. **robustness evidence**: do those differences persist across prompts, slices, seeds, or measurement choices?

If one of those layers is weak, the claim should be weaker too. That is less a limitation of the field than a reminder that mechanistic understanding is harder than benchmark improvement.

## Recap

To recap, here are the main interpretability questions this project focused on:

- **Output gains do not specify a mechanism.** Performance is the starting point for explanation.
- **Model comparison requires matched conditions.** Prompt selection, token budget, layer choice, and metric choice all matter.
- **Interpretability depends on evidence discipline.** A common outcome is a narrower hypothesis with better-calibrated confidence.

Earlier projects focus on optimization. The final project focuses on internal comparison and explanation.
