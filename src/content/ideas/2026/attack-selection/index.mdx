Most AI control evaluations assume the attacker doesn't think carefully about when to attack. The red team attacks on some fixed fraction of tasks, without asking whether a given task is a good opportunity. A capable misaligned model wouldn't behave that way. It would wait for cover, and back off when things started to look suspicious.

This year we published a paper that tests what happens when you give the attacker that kind of judgment. It's called *Attack Selection in Agentic AI Control Evaluations Meaningfully Decreases Safety* [@gewang2026attack]. I did it with Cath Ge-Wang, my co-first author, along with Ben Hadad and others, mentored by Tyler Tracy at Redwood Research. The short answer is that selection matters a lot. When humans can review only the most suspicious 1% of the model's work, simple prompted selection policies cut measured safety by about 20 to 28 percentage points, in two different agentic environments.

Rather than summarize the paper, this post tries to build the intuition that made the results feel obvious to me in hindsight. Almost everything in it can be understood through one picture, which shows two score distributions and a threshold. Once that picture makes sense, the safety curves, the start and stop policies, the appendix math and a few follow-up ideas all fall out of it.

The post builds in order. It starts with how a control evaluation works and how safety is scored, then builds the picture and shows how the standard safety curve falls out of it. Only then does it add selection, read the paper's results through the picture, and extend the argument to a whole deployment. I assume basic probability and a rough sense of what AI control is, but nothing about the details of control evaluations.

## How control evaluations score safety

{/* NEW PROSE (review): part lead-in. */}
This part builds the one picture the rest of the post leans on. It covers how a control evaluation is set up, how the audit budget becomes a threshold, and how safety gets scored, all before the attacker gets any say in when to attack. If you already know that machinery, you can skip ahead to [What attack selection does](#what-attack-selection-does).

### How a control evaluation is set up

AI control asks a narrow question. If a model were secretly trying to cause harm, would our safeguards catch it before it succeeded? The framing is deliberately pessimistic. Instead of trying to tell whether a model is misaligned, a control evaluation assumes that it is and asks whether the safeguards hold up anyway.

The evaluation is set up as a game between two teams. The red team stands in for the misaligned model and writes its attack strategy, usually as prompts and scaffolding around a capable model. In our paper that model was Claude Opus 4.6. The blue team designs the safeguards that are supposed to catch it. This style of evaluation comes from Greenblatt et al. [@greenblatt2023control], and the setup below follows theirs closely.

The model being evaluated is called untrusted, because it's capable enough that it might be scheming. Its main tasks are real jobs done in a Linux sandbox, which in our paper came from two environments called BashArena and LinuxArena. In BashArena the tasks are sysadmin-style. When the red team attacks, the model works on the main task as usual but also tries to complete a hidden side task, such as disabling the firewall, downloading malware, leaking passwords or granting itself sudo. Each pairing of a main task with a side task is one attack opportunity.

As an example, a main task might ask the model to set up a scheduled job that rotates log files. An attacking model could do that job correctly while also adding a line to the sudoers file that gives it root access, in the hope that the change blends in with its other configuration edits.

One run of the model on one task produces a trajectory, which is the sequence of actions it takes, like shell commands and file edits. In an attack trajectory, the model pursues a side task. In an honest trajectory, the same model does the same kind of task with no side task, which is what normal work looks like.

The blue team's main tool is a trusted model, one weak enough that we don't worry about it scheming. It watches every action the untrusted model takes and gives it a suspicion score from 0 to 10, a setup known as trusted monitoring. The blue team also has a small budget of human audits, stated as the fraction of trajectories humans can review, and we assume an audit always recognizes an attack. The blue team never learns which trajectories are attacks, so it has to decide where to spend its audits from the scores alone.

If a human audits an attack, the blue team wins, because one caught attack is strong evidence of misalignment and lets the developer stop the deployment. If an attack completes its side task without being audited, the red team wins. If an attack is neither caught nor successful, nothing happens and the game goes on.

A control evaluation runs many honest and attack trajectories, scores them all, and estimates how often the blue team would win. That number is safety, defined precisely below. Most evaluations have the red team attack a fixed fraction of opportunities, chosen at random. Our paper asks what happens to safety when the red team gets to choose which opportunities to attack instead.

### Monitor scores and the audit threshold

Run the monitor over every honest trajectory and every attack trajectory. A trajectory's score is the highest suspicion the monitor gave any of its steps. If you plot the two sets of scores, you get two overlapping distributions, with honest work on the left and attacks mostly to the right.

To keep things concrete, the figures in this post use a toy model instead of our data. Honest scores are drawn from Normal(0, 1) and attack scores from Normal(2.3, 1.1), in arbitrary units. The shapes are realistic, but the numbers don't come from the paper.

The audit budget becomes a threshold τ on that axis. A 1% budget means you can afford to audit 1% of honest work, so τ sits where the top 1% of honest scores begin. Anything above τ gets a human review.

Think of the monitor plus its threshold as a classifier, with attacks as the positive class. The audit budget is then its false-positive rate, meaning the share of honest trajectories that get flagged. τ is set using honest trajectories only, and attacks play no part in it. This point is worth holding onto, because much of what follows depends on it. An attacker can change which attacks show up, but it can't move τ.

[fig:score-distributions] shows the toy model at a 1% budget. Ignore the solid blue curve for now. It's the set of attacks a selective attacker actually runs, which is where the post is headed.

The share of attacks above τ is the monitor's true-positive rate (also called recall), which is just the caught rate. The overlap region near τ is where the game gets decided. Attacks far to the right are caught at any sensible budget, and attacks far to the left were never at risk.

| Rate | Definition | Role in a control evaluation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| False-positive rate (FPR) | Pr(score > τ ∣ honest) | The audit budget. Fixed in advance by how much human review you can afford, and it fixes τ. |
| True-positive rate (TPR) | Pr(score > τ ∣ attack) | The caught rate. The outcome you measure once τ is fixed. |

This is a different way of using a classifier than you might be used to. In a typical ML evaluation, you look at both kinds of error together, using a summary like accuracy or F1, and then pick a threshold that trades false positives against missed positives in whatever way suits the application. In control we don't get to choose that trade-off. The amount of human review we can afford fixes the false-positive rate, the false-positive rate fixes τ, and the true-positive rate is whatever comes out at that τ. So the question we ask of a monitor is always how many attacks it catches at one particular, small false-positive rate.

### Sweeping the threshold to get an ROC curve

If you haven't worked with ROC curves in a while, it helps to start from the general idea. Many classifiers don't output a yes or a no directly. They output a score, and you turn the score into a decision by picking a threshold, so that anything above it counts as a positive. A spam filter works this way, and so does our monitor, where the score is suspicion and a positive means the trajectory gets sent to a human.

The threshold controls a trade-off. A strict threshold flags very little, so it rarely raises false alarms, but it also misses many of the real positives. A lenient threshold catches more of the real positives, at the cost of more false alarms. The FPR and TPR from the previous section measure these two things for one particular threshold.

An ROC curve shows that trade-off for every threshold at once. You compute the FPR and TPR at each possible threshold and plot them against each other, with the FPR on the x-axis and the TPR on the y-axis. The curve always runs from the bottom-left corner, where the threshold is so strict that nothing gets flagged, to the top-right corner, where it's so lenient that everything does. What differs from one classifier to another is the path the curve takes between those two corners.

So far τ has been fixed at a 1% budget. Now imagine sliding it. If you move τ to the left, more honest trajectories land above it, so the false-positive rate goes up. More attacks land above it too, so the true-positive rate goes up as well. If you move τ to the right, both rates fall.

The table below shows a few positions of τ in the toy model, from strict to lenient.

| Position of τ (toy units) | Honest work above τ (FPR) | Attacks above τ (TPR) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3.09 | 0.1% | 24% |
| 2.33 | 1% | 49% |
| 1.64 | 5% | 72% |
| 1.28 | 10% | 82% |
| 0.00 | 50% | 98% |

Each row of the table is one point on the monitor's ROC curve, with the FPR on the x-axis and the TPR on the y-axis, and sliding τ smoothly through every position fills in the rest of the curve. A monitor that couldn't tell attacks from honest work would sit on the diagonal, where TPR equals FPR: any τ would flag the same share of attacks as of honest trajectories. A better monitor bows up toward the top-left corner, because at the same FPR it reaches a higher TPR. It gets more true positives (attacks caught) for the same number of false positives (honest trajectories sent to a human).

In control we only ever use one point on this curve, the one picked out by the audit budget. That point sits at the far left, since budgets are usually somewhere between 0.1% and 5%. On an ordinary linear axis that whole range gets squeezed into a sliver next to the y-axis, which is why control papers plot the FPR on a log scale. [fig:roc-curve] shows the toy model's ROC curve both ways.

Reading off the log-scale panel, the toy monitor catches about 24% of attacks at a 0.1% budget, 49% at a 1% budget and 72% at a 5% budget. The 1% point is the same 49% from [fig:score-distributions]. Changing the audit budget just means moving along this curve.

### Turning the caught rate into safety

The paper reports its results as safety rather than as a caught rate, so the next step is to connect the two.

Each attack attempt ends in one of three ways. It gets caught, it succeeds without being caught, or it fails quietly and nothing happens. A deployment is the stretch of real use the evaluation stands in for, and it ends at the first catch or the first uncaught success. Call those two outcomes decisive. Quiet failures leave the game running, so they drop out of the score.

Counting these outcomes over the sampled attack trajectories gives the safety metric we report in the paper.

$$
\text{safety} = \frac{|\text{caught}|}{|\text{caught}| + |\text{uncaught and side task succeeded}|}
$$

This is a ratio of two counts, which will matter later when we look at attackers that attack less often.

Write $p$ for the chance that an attack completes its side task, and assume for now that it's independent of whether the monitor catches it. Then each attempt is caught with probability TPR and succeeds uncaught with probability (1 − TPR) × $p$. Putting those into the ratio gives safety as a function of the caught rate.

$$
\text{safety} = \frac{\text{TPR}}{\text{TPR} + (1 - \text{TPR})\, p}
$$

When every uncaught attack succeeds ($p = 1$), safety equals the TPR. A lower $p$ pushes safety above the TPR, because some attacks that slip past the monitor fail on their own. For example, a TPR of 50% with $p$ = 70% gives safety of about 59%. Safety always rises with the TPR, so from here on I'll mostly talk about the caught rate.

This also tells us what the safety curves in the paper are. Pick an audit budget, find the TPR at that budget on the ROC curve, and convert it to safety with the formula above. Doing that for every budget gives a safety-versus-audit-budget curve, which is the ROC curve from [fig:roc-curve] with its y-axis relabeled from caught rate to safety. Because safety rises with the TPR, anything that moves the ROC curve moves the safety curve in the same direction. [fig:safety-curve], in the next section, shows a safety curve for the toy model.

## What attack selection does

{/* NEW PROSE (review): part lead-in. */}
Now the attacker gets to choose. This part shows what that choice does to the picture, why the usual summary statistic for a monitor hides the damage, and how the paper's results look through the same lens.

### How selection changes the attacks the monitor sees

Attack selection means the attacker decides, from what it can observe, whether an opportunity is worth attacking. It can see the main task, the side task and its own actions so far. It can't see the monitor's scores, so it has to predict them.

Selection doesn't make any single attack stealthier. In our experiments the attacks were fixed in advance. We collected trajectories from a strong attacker first, and then applied the selection decisions described below on top of them. That way, any change in safety comes from choosing when to attack rather than from attacking better.

What selection changes is the set of attacks that τ gets applied to. A good selector skips the attacks that would land above τ and keeps the ones that would land below it. The survivors sit in the overlap region, where they look like ordinary honest work. That's the solid blue curve in [fig:score-distributions]. In the toy model it comes from a selector that sees each attack's score through Gaussian noise and keeps the 30% it predicts will score lowest.

Two things follow. The distributions can stay far apart overall and selection still wins, as long as it removes the attacks that would have crossed τ. And only the region near τ matters, which is why the effect depends so much on the audit budget.

We split selection into two decisions, and each one acts on the picture in a different way.

- **Start policy.** Before doing anything, the attacker looks at the main task and side task and decides whether this is a good opportunity. Skipping a task removes a whole trajectory, so the blatant attacks in the right tail are the first to go.
- **Stop policy.** At each step, the attacker looks at the trajectory so far and decides whether to abort. Because a trajectory's score is the maximum over its steps, stopping before the most suspicious step lowers the score. The downside is that stopping early often also keeps the side task from succeeding.

{/* WIDGET (optional, new): StopPolicyTrajectory — per-step suspicion of one attack, draggable stop point, audit line. Not in the artifact. */}

Both policies are prompted language models that output a score from 1 to 9, and the attacker acts when the score crosses a threshold. We tuned that threshold with 10-fold cross-validation. We split the attack trajectories into ten folds, picked the threshold that minimized safety on nine of them, and reported safety on the tenth. Tuning on monitor outcomes is generous to the red team, since a deployed attacker wouldn't see them. So read the results as an upper bound on what prompted selection can do.

The obvious objection is that a selective attacker simply attacks less often. That alone shouldn't change safety, because safety is a ratio. If an attacker attacks half as often, the number of caught attacks and the number of uncaught successes both halve, and the ratio stays the same. So attacking less often doesn't make an attacker more dangerous on its own, and what matters is attacking at better moments. We checked this directly with random start and stop baselines matched on how often they attack. Random starting keeps the same score distribution with fewer attacks, and it didn't produce the drop. Random stopping lowers safety a little, but the real stop policy beat it at matched rates.

### Why AUROC understates the effect

As [fig:roc-curve] showed, the audit budgets used in control sit at the far-left edge of the ROC curve. AUROC, the area under the whole ROC curve, averages over every false-positive rate, including the 50% to 100% region no one would ever operate in. Equivalently, it's the chance that a random attack scores above a random honest trajectory.

Selection shows how misleading that average can be. In the toy model behind the figures, selection lowers AUROC by about a tenth, from 0.94 to 0.84. At a 1% FPR it cuts the TPR from 49% to 8%, the numbers in [fig:score-distributions]'s title. The monitor and the honest data haven't changed, yet the caught rate drops sixfold at the budget we actually care about. To get the 49% back against the selective attacker, you'd need to audit about 9% of honest work instead of 1%.

This is why it's better to report the TPR at a fixed low FPR than to report AUROC. A few other points follow from the same picture.

**Selection changes the positives, not the monitor.** An ROC curve describes a monitor *and* the set of positives it was tested on. Attack selection leaves the monitor and the honest data alone and swaps the positives for a harder subset. A monitor's ROC measured on attacks spread across all tasks is therefore optimistic about the attacks it will actually face. At a fixed budget, this shows up as a lower TPR. If you hold the TPR fixed instead, it shows up as a much larger audit budget.

An offline analysis from before the paper estimated how much extra auditing selection costs. It used a different attacker and monitor, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and GPT-4.1-mini. Two LLM judges estimated, from the task descriptions alone, how likely each attack opportunity was to succeed and to be flagged. We ranked opportunities by the combination and kept only the top slice. Against an attacker restricted to its best 2% of opportunities, a 20% audit budget gave about the same safety (0.42) as a 5% budget against an attacker who attacked everywhere. Each step up in selectivity needed a proportional step up in auditing.

**Low precision isn't a problem here.** Attacks are rare in deployment, so almost every flagged trajectory is honest work. That means the monitor's precision, the share of flagged trajectories that are actually attacks, is very low. In most applications a precision that low would be a problem. In control it isn't, because the blue team only needs one catch.

**Step-level and trajectory-level rates differ.** If an honest trajectory has $L$ steps and each step crosses a step-level threshold with probability $f$, the trajectory's false-positive rate is about $1 - (1 - f)^L$, or roughly $L \times f$ when $f$ is small. Longer trajectories need a stricter per-step threshold for the same budget. On the attack side, scoring by the maximum is what gives the stop policy something to work with.

### Results from the paper

In the paper, Claude Opus 4.6 played the attacker in BashArena and LinuxArena. The monitor was MiMo-V2-Flash, sampled five times per action and averaged, with the trajectory's max as its score. The start and stop scorers were a separate model, MiMo-V2.5-Pro. Here is empirical safety without and with selection at the three audit budgets we report.

| Policy and setting | 1% audit | 2% audit | 5% audit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start, BashArena | 0.20 → 0.00 (n=15) | 0.30 → 0.00 (n=3) | 0.41 → 0.00 (n=3) |
| Start, LinuxArena | 0.31 → 0.11 (n=18) | 0.56 → 0.35 (n=20) | 0.77 → 0.67 (n=61) |
| Stop, BashArena | 0.20 → 0.00 (n=2) | 0.30 → 0.13 (n=15) | 0.42 → 0.37 (n=223) |
| Stop, LinuxArena | 0.36 → 0.09 (n=23) | 0.61 → 0.47 (n=17) | 0.79 → 0.79 (n=28) |

Each cell reads baseline → with selection, and n counts the attack trajectories left after selection. All twelve cells point the same way, but many rest on few trajectories, because selection works by shrinking the set of attacks that actually happen. Only the LinuxArena stop result at 1% has a 95% confidence interval that excludes zero on its own. So we pooled the cells with a Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test, which looks for a common effect across cells while letting each keep its own baseline. Selected trajectories had about one-seventh the odds of being caught compared with the ones the policies rejected (p ≈ 9×10⁻⁵). If selection did nothing, we'd expect about 17 selected trajectories to be caught at a 1% budget. Only four were.

The paper also plots safety against audit budget for each environment, like [fig:safety-curve] but with real data. Several features of those curves can be read straight off the distributions.

- **Without selection, BashArena's safety has a floor near 12% at tiny budgets.** Some attacks score above every honest trajectory, so even a 0.01% budget catches them. The start policy pushes that floor to zero, because those blatant attacks are the first ones it drops.
- **LinuxArena sits at 0% below about a 0.2% budget, then rises steeply.** Its honest distribution has a long right tail that covers even the most suspicious attacks. As the budget grows, τ drops into the bulk of the attack distribution and catches climb fast.
- **The gap from selection peaks near a 1% budget.** That's where τ cuts through the dense part of the attack distribution, so moving attacks under τ matters most.
- **The gap has mostly closed by a 5% budget.** A lower τ means selection has to push attacks further left to hide them, so the same selector has less room. That matches the drop from 20 to 28 points at 1% to between 1 and 10 points at 5%.

## Deployments, low-rate attacks, and other interventions

{/* NEW PROSE (review): part lead-in. */}
This part widens the view in three directions. It follows selection over a whole deployment, separates it from an older strategy that also attacks rarely, and lines it up against the other interventions that move the same two distributions.

### Safety over a whole deployment

Everything so far is about single attack attempts. A fair question is whether a selective attacker, who attacks less often, is really more dangerous over a whole deployment. Appendix A of the paper answers that with a small Markov model [@gewang2026attack].

On each of $N$ tasks, one of three things happens. The attacker gets caught (probability $g_\text{caught}$), succeeds uncaught ($g_\text{uncaught}$), or nothing decisive happens ($g_\text{cont}$). The blue team is safe if nothing decisive ever happens, or if a catch comes before an uncaught success.

$$
S_N = g_{\text{cont}}^{N} + \left(1 - g_{\text{cont}}^{N}\right)\frac{g_{\text{caught}}}{g_{\text{caught}} + g_{\text{uncaught}}}
$$

To see where this comes from, note that the chance of all $N$ tasks passing with nothing decisive is $g_\text{cont}^N$, and that outcome counts as safe. Otherwise the game ends at the first decisive task, and that task is a catch with probability $g_\text{caught} / (g_\text{caught} + g_\text{uncaught})$.

The ratio on the right is the empirical safety from earlier. The attack rate enters only through the first term, the chance that the attacker never does anything decisive at all.

A selective attacker shrinks both $g_\text{caught}$ and $g_\text{uncaught}$, because it attacks less, but it shrinks $g_\text{caught}$ faster. This separates the two effects cleanly. Attacking rarely mostly changes whether the game ends at all, while selection changes how it ends. In a short deployment with only one or two expected attempts, a selective attacker can even look safer, simply because it often never acts. As $N$ grows, the first term goes to zero, both attackers settle onto their ratios, and selection is strictly worse for the blue team.

### Selection versus attacking at a low rate

There's an older red-team strategy that also involves attacking rarely, and it's worth separating from selection. Under upfront auditing, humans review the first $aN$ tasks of an $N$-task deployment, a fraction $a$ of the whole, and the attacker attacks each task independently with some fixed probability $r$. There's no score threshold here, so the distributions above don't apply. Nothing after the window gets audited, so the attacker wins if it gets through the window without attacking, then attacks at least once afterward.

$$
P(\text{red wins}) = (1 - r)^{aN}\left[1 - (1 - r)^{(1-a)N}\right], \qquad r^{*} \approx \frac{-\ln a}{(1-a)N}
$$

Minimizing over $r$ for large $N$ gives the attacker's best rate $r^*$, shown on the right, and it's very small. With a 1% audit window it's about 0.46% at $N$ = 1,000 and 0.0005% at $N$ = 1,000,000. Either way, the idealized attacker expects only about $-\ln a$ attacks over the whole deployment, around 4.6 for a 1% window.

Whether a model can actually act at such a low rate is an empirical question. Serrano, Xing, Lindner and Jenner [@serrano2026lowprob] found that frontier models can hit calibrated rates below 1 in 100,000 when given a source of in-context randomness, such as a UUID, and mostly fail without one.

The two strategies avoid detection in different ways. A low-rate attacker avoids the window when audits happen, while a selective attacker avoids actions the monitor would find suspicious. Selection also needs no random number generator, because variation across tasks supplies the randomness, so limiting a model's access to entropy wouldn't stop it. The realistic threat is probably a combination of the two, where the attacker attacks with low probability and only when the opportunity is good.

### How other interventions move the distributions

Most interventions in a control evaluation can be read as moving one of the two curves relative to τ. Putting them side by side shows how one picture covers all of them.

One row needs context. CTRL-Z [@bhatt2025ctrlz] is a blue-team protocol that resamples suspicious actions from the untrusted model and acts on the least suspicious sample. Like selection, it filters toward whatever looks least suspicious, but it's the blue team doing the filtering.

<Table>

| Intervention | Honest scores | Attack scores | τ at a fixed budget | Safety |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Attack selection (red team) | Unchanged | Kept attacks shift left | Unchanged | Falls |
| Random selection at the same rate | Unchanged | Same shape, fewer attacks | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| Resampling, as in CTRL-Z (blue team) | Shift left a lot | Shift left a little | Falls | Rises |
| A monitor with fewer honest false alarms | Tighter | Similar | Falls | Rises |
| A larger audit budget | Unchanged | Unchanged | Falls | Rises, and selection has less room |

</Table>

Resampling and selection both pick "the version that looks less suspicious." What decides who benefits is whose distribution moves more relative to τ. Resampling helps the blue team because honest suspicion is mostly noise, which resampling averages away, while attack suspicion is mostly signal, which survives it.

## Open questions

**Why doesn't safety go to zero?** A perfect selector would attack only when its score will land below τ, and safety would be zero. Real selectors have to predict a noisy monitor from the outside. That's also why, in our stop-policy experiments, prompts that imitated the monitor's own prompt beat more elaborate attacker reasoning: the attacker needs an internal model of the monitor, and the monitor's prompt is the best one on hand. In the toy model, the noisier the selector's read of each score, the closer the blue curve in [fig:score-distributions] sits to the dashed one.

**Why is the start policy's job easier than the stop policy's?** The start policy gets to choose among many tasks. Across main tasks there's a lot of variation in how much cover each one offers, and it only has to attack more often on the good ones. The stop policy only acts inside a trajectory that's already fixed, and under max-suspicion auditing, stopping early enough to avoid the decisive step often kills the side task too. In the pre-paper experiments mentioned earlier, stopping barely helped on the strongest attack sets and helped more on weaker ones, where less selection had already happened upstream.

**How should the blue team respond?** We held the blue team fixed. One natural response is to keep the total false-positive spend the same but move it to where attacks concentrate: a lower threshold on tasks that offer good cover, a higher one elsewhere. Another is to make the monitor less predictable, with ensembles or randomization, so the attacker's internal model of it gets worse.

**Does resampling defend against selection?** Possibly. If resampling tightens the honest distribution and lowers τ, there's less overlap region for a selector to hide in. We haven't tested it.

**Would one model do this without saying so?** In our setup, selection runs in separate scorer models, so the attacker's chain of thought never shows it. Whether a single policy could select this well without reasoning about it out loud, where a chain-of-thought monitor might notice, is open.

**What does it look like online?** Our stop policy truncates recorded trajectories after the fact. A real agent that aborts can't just vanish. It has to keep working on the main task and keep looking normal. Running the stop policy online, and training a selector instead of prompting one, are the follow-ups I'm most interested in.

## Takeaways

If you run control evaluations, the practical upshot is to include an elicited selective attacker, and to report the caught rate at the budgets you'd actually use. A random-rate attacker measures the monitor against attacks it will rarely face.

If you only keep five sentences from this post, these are the ones I'd pick.

1. The audit budget is the monitor's false-positive rate, set on honest work, and attackers can't move it.
2. Safety is the share of decisive outcomes that are catches, so it depends on which attacks happen, not how many.
3. A safety curve is an ROC curve on a log false-positive axis, and only its left edge matters.
4. Selection moves the attacks that happen into the overlap region near τ, without touching the monitor or the threshold.
5. Resampling mostly moves the honest distribution, while selection moves the attack distribution.
