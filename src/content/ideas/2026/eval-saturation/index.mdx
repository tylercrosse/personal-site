Benchmark curves keep tracing the same shape: a long stretch near the floor, a steep run through the middle, then years of expensive grinding just below the ceiling. Each phase gets read as news about the models, a breakthrough when the curve turns up and a wall when it flattens. GPQA went from stumping frontier models to saturated in about two years, and both ends of that run produced their own narratives [@burnham2026rip]. I think the shape is mostly arithmetic, and the arithmetic fits in two lines.

A task that takes 100 steps, each done right 99% of the time, succeeds 37% of the time.

Make each step 99.9% reliable, one more nine, and the task succeeds 90% of the time.

That is a 54-point benchmark jump from one steady 10× reduction in per-step error. The model improved smoothly the whole way; the exponent did the rest.

None of the parts here are original, and the sources are worth naming up front. Sinha et al. built a benchmark around exactly this compounding and argue it makes scaling look better than headline scores suggest [@sinha2025illusion]. Schaeffer, Miranda, and Koyejo used the same compounding to argue that "emergent abilities" are mostly artifacts of metric choice [@schaeffer2023mirage]. Ord turned it into a survival model for AI agents, which builds on METR's time-horizon measurements [@ord2025halflife; @kwa2025measuring]. What I want to add is smaller: these are one equation wearing three different morals, and the equation makes a testable prediction about where eval curves should inflect.

## One equation

Call the chance that one step goes right $n$. A task that takes $p$ steps succeeds only when every step does, so its success rate is

$$
n^p.
$$

That exponent is the entire mechanism behind the opening numbers: $0.99^{100} \approx 37\%$, and $0.999^{100} \approx 90\%$. When $p$ is large, small movements in $n$ produce large movements in $n^p$.

Scaling enters through $n$. Per-step error, $\varepsilon = 1 - n$, falls as a power law in compute:

$$
\varepsilon(C) = \varepsilon_0\, C^{-\alpha}.
$$

Here $C$ is compute, measured in multiples of some reference model's budget; $\varepsilon_0$ is that reference model's per-step error, where the curve starts; and $\alpha$ is how fast error falls with scale: cutting error by 10× always costs the same factor of compute, $10^{1/\alpha}$. This is the boring, well-attested part; smooth power-law improvement is what scaling laws describe.

Substitute $n = 1 - \varepsilon(C)$ into $n^p$ and the score of a $p$-step task becomes a function of compute:

$$
S(C) = \left(1 - \varepsilon_0 C^{-\alpha}\right)^p.
$$

A benchmark is a bag of tasks with different $p$, so its score averages these curves over a task-length distribution. The sigmoid appears when the exponent meets the falling error rate.

Three things fall out:

- Plotted against log-compute, $S$ is an S-curve whose steepest point sits at a score of $1/e \approx 37\%$ regardless of task length (exactly $(1 - 1/p)^p$, which converges to $1/e$ as $p$ grows).
- Task length only slides the curve sideways. Doubling $p$ costs a constant factor $2^{1/\alpha}$ of compute, so the task horizon a model can handle grows exponentially under steady scaling. This is the same arithmetic behind METR's doubling time horizons [@kwa2025measuring].
- Mixing task lengths smears the crisp S into the familiar saturation grind. Short tasks are solved early and prop up the low end; the longest tasks hold the ceiling out of reach for years.

## Three papers, one equation

Part of why I trust this frame is that three groups derived it independently and pointed it in three directions.

Sinha et al. call the pessimistic reading an illusion [@sinha2025illusion]. When single-step accuracy is already high, further gains look like diminishing returns (99.2% to 99.8% reads as noise), but the task horizon that accuracy supports grows exponentially. Their experiments isolate execution: hand the model the plan and the knowledge, then count how many steps it carries out before failing. Small models with near-perfect single-step accuracy still fall apart quickly, and larger models execute far longer tasks. Their moral: scaling is underrated.

Schaeffer, Miranda, and Koyejo run the same compounding in reverse [@schaeffer2023mirage]. A model whose per-token accuracy improves smoothly looks flat on an exact-match, multi-token metric until the compounding crosses over, at which point the score leaps and someone declares emergence. Score the same outputs with a metric that is linear in per-token error and the leap disappears. Their moral: breakthroughs are overrated.

Ord reads it as actuarial science [@ord2025halflife]. Treat each unit of task time as carrying a constant hazard of fatal failure and you get an exponentially declining success rate in task length, a characteristic half-life per agent, and a clean fit to METR's data [@kwa2025measuring]. No moral at all, just a hazard rate.

All three are right at once, which is the point. The information was never in the benchmark score; it is in the per-step error rate, and the score is a lens over it with a known distortion. Whether you call the distortion an illusion of diminishing returns or a mirage of emergence depends on which end of the lens you are looking through.

## The same curve in the wild

Epoch's Benchmarking Hub publishes score-by-model data for dozens of benchmarks [@epochbenchmarkinghub], which makes the model cheap to check against reality. Here are five frontier trajectories, picked to span the stages:

<BenchmarkStages />

MATH Level 5 is finished. It crossed 37% in late 2023, hit 95% within about a year, and has gained a third of a point since spring 2025. GPQA Diamond is deep in the grind: 87% in mid-2025, 95% today. FrontierMath and Humanity's Last Exam are in the steep middle and have more than doubled in the past year. And the Remote Labor Index is where every benchmark starts, at 1% a year ago and 16% now, still below the turn.

The model puts the steepest part of every curve at 37% of ceiling, and the crossings came in pairs: GPT-4 Turbo carried MATH Level 5 and GPQA Diamond past that line on the same day in November 2023, and Gemini 3 Pro carried FrontierMath and Humanity's Last Exam past it on the same day in November 2025.

The chart and the equation run on different axes, so the tie between them is worth making explicit. The equation runs on compute; the chart runs on dates. Frontier training compute has grown at a roughly steady exponential rate, which makes calendar time a stand-in for log-compute. Substitute that in, take the large-$p$ limit, and the whole model collapses to two numbers per benchmark:

$$
S(t) = e^{-e^{-r\,(t - t^{*})}}.
$$

Everything from the derivation is still inside. Task length $p$ and starting error $\varepsilon_0$ merge into $t^{*}$, the date the benchmark crosses 1/e: longer tasks and harder problems mean a later $t^{*}$. The scaling exponent $\alpha$ and the compute growth rate merge into $r$, the climb rate.

The dashed curves in the chart are that two-parameter form, fit to each frontier[^fit-method]:

| Benchmark             | Crosses 1/e             | r (per year) | Implied α | R²   |
| --------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------ | --------- | ---- |
| MATH Level 5          | Dec 2023                | 1.50         | 1.00      | 0.79 |
| GPQA Diamond          | Apr 2024                | 1.28         | 0.85      | 0.97 |
| FrontierMath          | Oct 2025                | 1.00         | 0.66      | 0.98 |
| Humanity's Last Exam  | Nov 2025                | 1.00         | 0.66      | 0.96 |
| Remote Labor Index    | Mar 2027 (extrapolated) | 0.98         | 0.65      | 0.84 |

GPQA Diamond's fit includes its 25% multiple-choice floor. The three tightest fits share nearly the same climb rate, so those curves really do differ mainly in $t^{*}$. The R² column deserves a caveat, though: a logistic would track these frontiers about as well. Goodness of fit alone cannot separate the two families; they diverge at the inflection height, and that comparison is the next section's job.

The implied-$\alpha$ column converts each climb rate back into the equation's terms. Frontier training compute has grown about 4.5× per year [@sevilla2024compute], and with time standing in for log-compute the two rates are tied by $r = \alpha \ln 4.5$. The fitted values land between 0.65 and 1.0, ordinary scaling-exponent territory; treat them as upper bounds, since algorithmic progress makes effective compute grow faster than training compute alone, which shrinks the implied $\alpha$. At $r \approx 1$ the climb from 10% to 90% takes about three years, roughly two orders of magnitude of compute.

Two rows need caveats. MATH Level 5 fits worst because the jump when reasoning models arrived in September 2024 was a genuine change in rate, and a single $r$ cannot absorb it. And the Remote Labor Index row is an extrapolation: the fit puts its 1/e crossing in March 2027, a dated prediction this post can be checked against.

[^fit-method]: Least-squares Gompertz fits to the frontier points, grid-searched over $t^{*}$ and $r$; floors are zero except GPQA's 25%. Two limitations to keep in mind: frontier points are running maxima, so the fits select on lucky runs and read slightly high, and no uncertainty is reported, though a dozen points per benchmark implies real error bars on both parameters. Implied $\alpha$ uses the midpoint of Epoch's 4–5×-per-year range; using 4× or 5× instead moves it by under 10%.

## The check: 37% or 50%?

The model makes one prediction that standard practice quietly contradicts. When people fit benchmark trajectories they usually reach for a logistic, and a logistic inflects at half its ceiling. Compounding says a fixed-length task family follows a Gompertz-type sigmoid instead, with its steepest point near $1/e$, about 37% of the ceiling. Fastest progress should arrive well before the halfway mark, and the top half of the curve should take longer than the bottom half.

Epoch's Benchmarking Hub publishes model-by-model scores across dozens of benchmarks under CC BY [@epochbenchmarkinghub], which is enough to test this: fit both curve families, compare inflection heights, and account for the chance floor on multiple-choice suites. Two wrinkles make it a genuine test. Mixing task lengths pushes the steepest point back up toward the middle (the benchmark-mix view above shows this happening), so the prediction is sharpest for benchmarks with narrow task-length distributions, like Sinha et al.'s fixed-turn execution suites, and blurriest for grab-bag agentic evals. And placing many benchmarks on a common difficulty scale is its own hard problem, which Ho et al. treat properly [@ho2025rosetta].

The dashed curves above show the Gompertz family tracking five frontiers I picked by hand; a fair test fits both families across the whole Hub and compares. I have not run that yet. This section will hold the result when I have it, including a negative one: if eval curves inflect at 50% even after controlling for mixture, the simple compounding story is missing something important.

## Where the model breaks

The equation assumes steps fail independently, and they don't.

Failures correlate. One bad early decision, a wrong plan or a misread file, poisons every step after it, which makes a long task behave like a shorter task with a worse per-step rate. Sinha et al. document a sharper version they call self-conditioning: models become more likely to err after seeing their own earlier errors in context, so the per-step rate drifts upward within a task instead of holding constant [@sinha2025illusion].

Recovery cuts the other way. Real agents retry and backtrack, and a reanalysis of METR's data by Gus Hamilton found that hazard rates decline over the course of a task: an agent that survives the opening minutes is disproportionately likely to finish.[^hamilton-reanalysis] That is what you would expect if some tasks, or some starts, are simply harder, and it complicates the constant hazard in Ord's original model.

[^hamilton-reanalysis]: [Ord summarizes Hamilton's reanalysis here](https://x.com/tobyordoxford/status/2018986170324054064).

So the $p$ in the equation is an effective step count, shrunk by correlated failures and stretched by recovery. I treat the model the way you would treat an ideal gas law: wrong in the details but right about which variables matter.

## What this changes about reading benchmarks

A benchmark's useful life is roughly computable. Given a scaling exponent and a task-length distribution, the time from "frontier models get traction" to "saturated" is a number, and it is short: the steep middle of the sigmoid spans a couple of orders of magnitude of effective compute. The goalposts move on a schedule, and retiring GPQA-class suites in favor of longer-horizon ones [@burnham2026rip] is what that schedule looks like from the inside.

Score deltas compress at both ends of the scale. A 2-point gain at 95% is a much larger reliability improvement than a 10-point gain at 50%; per-step error (the nines view in the widget) is the honest axis. METR's choice to report time horizon at a fixed success rate instead of score on a fixed suite [@kwa2025measuring] is the same correction applied at the other end.

A "sudden" jump on an eval is usually steady progress after the exponent is done with it.

If you have seen the inflection check done properly, or you know a benchmark whose curve clearly inflects at 50% after accounting for mixture and the chance floor, I want to know; the comments are open.
