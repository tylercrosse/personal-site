A pretrained language model has read much of the internet and still can't take a request. It completes documents. If you type "What is the capital of France?", a reasonable continuation is another quiz question, since on the web that line usually appears in a list of them. The model probably knows the answer. Answering you just isn't what it's been trained to do.

Post-training is everything done after pretraining to change that. It turns a document completer into something you can hand a task to: a model that answers the question, follows instructions, holds a conversation, calls tools, works through a hard problem step by step, and declines some requests. InstructGPT made the point early: labelers preferred outputs from a 1.3B-parameter post-trained model over those from the 175B-parameter GPT-3, a model more than 100 times larger [@ouyang2022instructgpt].

For a while the short version was that post-training *elicits* what pretraining already put there. A thin layer of instruction tuning and preference tuning chose which of the many voices in the base model you'd hear. That story is less true now. Reinforcement learning (RL) on problems with checkable answers has become a main way that models get better at math, code, and long agentic tasks [@deepseek2025r1], so post-training is increasingly where the model learns to do the task as well as how to present it.

I've come to think of them as lego: standard pieces that snap together in different ways. Each training stage combines three of them:

</MapStep>

- **Data**, which comes from somewhere (people, other models, programs, the model itself) and has some shape (a demonstration, a comparison, a task with a grader).

</MapStep>

- **A learning signal** derived from that data, which tells the model what better looks like. For a reward, something has to do the scoring, such as a reward model, a model judge or a program. That's the map's "turned into a reward by" column.

</MapStep>

- **An update rule** that turns the signal into a change in the weights.

</MapStep>

A recipe is a particular assembly of those parts. Labs rearrange them every few months, a growing industry sells them, and the choices show up in how the finished model behaves.

## What a pipeline looks like in 2026

<PipelineFigure
  id="pipeline"

The top row is pretraining and its tail end. Midtraining, which some recipes add, continues next-token prediction on a mix weighted toward math, code, and instruction-like text. It blurs the boundary: by the time a base model exists, it has already seen a lot of data shaped like what post-training will ask of it.

Everything below the base model is post-training, and it has three broad moves.

The first is a cold start with supervised fine-tuning (SFT). The model trains on examples of the behavior you want: conversations, worked solutions, tool-use transcripts. This teaches format and gets the model to a point where its own attempts are good enough to learn from, which is why it's called a cold start for the RL that follows. DeepSeek showed what happens without it. R1-Zero, trained with RL straight from the base model, learned to reason well but wrote hard-to-read output that mixed languages. For R1, a small cold-start SFT stage, plus a reward during RL for staying in one language, largely fixed this [@deepseek2025r1].

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="lens=pipes&recipes=r1,k3,nemotron&hl=f-reward,m-grpo,m-ppo"
  desc="the same three pipelines with their RL stages lit: R1's two GRPO stages, K3's nine specialists, and Nemotron's RL stage over all environments followed by its specialist teachers.">

The second is reinforcement learning, increasingly split across specialists. Nemotron 3 Ultra runs one RL stage over all its training environments at once, using RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR), where a program checks each answer. It also trains more than ten specialist teachers, each given extra SFT or RL on its own domain [@nvidia2026nemotron3ultra]. Kimi K3 trains experts for general tasks, general agents, and coding agents, each at three reasoning-effort levels, for nine teachers in total [@kimi2026k3].

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="lens=pipes&recipes=r1,k3,nemotron&hl=m-opd"
  desc="the same three pipelines with the on-policy distillation stages lit: one at the end of Kimi K3, two at the end of Nemotron 3 Ultra.">

The third is consolidation by distillation. The specialists are merged back into one model with multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). The student generates its own responses, and the specialist for that domain scores every token of them. Nemotron 3 Ultra does this twice, branching new teachers from the distilled student for the second round [@nvidia2026nemotron3ultra].

</MapStep>

Not every recent recipe looks like this. The map now shows seven published ones, simplified. They all start with SFT and then diverge.

</MapStep>

InstructGPT (2022) has the classic RLHF shape: humans write demonstrations and compare outputs [@ouyang2022instructgpt]. DeepSeek-R1 (2025) alternates imitation and exploration, and its middle SFT stage is built from the RL model's own best outputs [@deepseek2025r1].

</MapStep>

Qwen3 (2025) merges thinking and non-thinking behavior, answering with or without a reasoning trace, into one model, and its smaller models are distilled from larger ones [@qwen2025qwen3]. Kimi K2 (2025) has one RL stage mixing verifiable rewards with rubric-based self-critique, where the model grades its own answers [@kimi2025k2].

</MapStep>

Olmo 3 Think (2025) has fully open data and code, and keeps a preference-tuning stage before RL [@olmo2025olmo3]. Kimi K3 and Nemotron 3 Ultra (2026) train narrow experts, then consolidate them; Nemotron also runs one RL stage over all environments first [@kimi2026k3; @nvidia2026nemotron3ultra].

The labels in those recipes (SFT, DPO, RL, distillation) are names for the parts. To see why they can be swapped and recombined, it helps to sort them by what the model is learning from and how it updates.

## Methods, sorted by what the model learns from

<ScrollyMap
  fallback={mapSignals}
  fallbackAlt="The four learning signals and the update rules each feeds: demonstrations to SFT; preferences to DPO, IPO, KTO and SimPO; scalar rewards to REINFORCE, RLOO, PPO, GRPO, GSPO and CISPO, fed by a reward model (RLHF), an LLM judge (RLAIF) and a program verifier (RLVR); teacher distributions to offline and on-policy distillation.">

<MapStep
  dwell="long"
  state="hl=family&zoom=scorer,family,method"
  desc="the four learning signals lit, with the update rules each one feeds to the right and the scorers to the left.">

There are dozens of named post-training algorithms, and most of the names describe small variations. The more useful question is what tells the model how to improve. There are four broad answers.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=f-demo,m-sft,m-rs&zoom=scorer,family,method"
  desc="demonstrations lit, leading to SFT and rejection sampling.">

**Demonstrations: "respond like this."** The model sees a prompt and a target response, and the update raises the probability of each token in the target. This is SFT, and it's the same next-token objective as pretraining, applied to curated text. Rejection sampling is a variant: generate several candidates, keep the ones a grader likes, and run SFT on the keepers. The grader chooses the data, but it never enters the gradient.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=f-pref,m-dpo,m-ipo,m-kto,m-simpo,s-rm&zoom=scorer,family,method"
  desc="preferences lit, leading to DPO, IPO, KTO and SimPO, with the reward model also lit.">

**Preferences: "this one, not that one."** The model sees a prompt with two responses, one marked better. Direct preference optimization (DPO) and its relatives push up the probability of the chosen response relative to the rejected one [@rafailov2023dpo]. This is preference tuning. You don't have to say what a perfect answer looks like, only which of two is better, which is often much easier for a person to judge. KTO, one of those relatives, works from single responses labelled good or bad, with no pairing [@ethayarajh2024kto]. The same comparisons can instead train a separate reward model, which is how classical RLHF used them.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=f-reward,m-reinforce,m-rloo,m-ppo,m-grpo,m-gspo,m-cispo,s-rm,s-judge,s-verifier&labels=1&zoom=scorer,family,method"
  desc="scalar rewards lit, leading to REINFORCE, RLOO, PPO, GRPO, GSPO and CISPO. Three scorers feed it: a reward model (labelled RLHF), an LLM judge with a rubric (RLAIF, rubric rewards) and a program verifier (RLVR).">

**Scalar rewards: "that attempt scored 0.7."** Now the model generates its own attempts, something scores each one, and the update makes above-average attempts more likely and below-average ones less likely. This is reinforcement learning. In RL terms the model being trained is the policy, and each attempt is a rollout. The scorer can be a reward model trained on human preferences (RLHF), a model acting as judge (RL from AI feedback, or RLAIF [@bai2022constitutional]), or a program that checks the answer, such as unit tests or a math answer key (RLVR [@lambert2024tulu3]). Those three acronyms name where the reward comes from. The optimizer can be the same in all three.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=f-teacher,m-offdistill,m-opd,s-teacher&labels=1&zoom=scorer,family,method"
  desc="teacher distributions lit, fed by a live teacher and leading to offline and on-policy distillation.">

**Teacher distributions: "here's what I would have said, token by token."** A stronger model, the teacher, supplies its probabilities for each next token, and the student learns to match them. In offline distillation the student trains on text the teacher wrote, which amounts to SFT on teacher outputs. In on-policy distillation the student writes the text, and the teacher scores each token by how likely the teacher would have been to write it. Kimi K3, for example, uses the log of the ratio between the teacher's and the student's probability for each token as a per-token reward [@kimi2026k3].

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  dwell="long"
  state="lens=grid"
  desc="the map switches to a two-by-two grid. Columns: signal on every token (dense), one signal per response (sparse). Rows: someone else's text (off-policy), the model's own text (on-policy). Each update rule sits in its cell, except rejection sampling and online DPO, which sit on the line between the rows. Notes under the grid say why: they train on the model's own samples, but in batches or rounds. They also say density partly belongs to the scorer: a process reward model makes any RL method step-dense.">

### The update rule: whose text, and how much signal

The third part, the update rule, is where I expected the differences to be: different loss functions. In practice two other properties separate the families more, and both are about what the update sees rather than the formula it applies.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="lens=grid&cell=bl,br&hl=m-opd,m-reinforce,m-rloo,m-ppo,m-grpo,m-gspo,m-cispo"
  desc="the grid's bottom row lit: on-policy distillation and the RL methods, which learn from the model's own text.">

The first is **whose text the model is learning from**. In SFT, the training text was written by someone else: a person, a teacher model, or an earlier version of the model. That's called off-policy. In RL, the model learns from its own fresh attempts, which is on-policy. On-policy learning matters because the model gets feedback on the mistakes it actually makes, including the ones that only show up halfway through a long answer it wrote itself. DPO usually trains on a fixed set of pairs, so I file it as off-policy, but that depends on where the pairs come from. Tülu 3, a fully open post-training recipe from Ai2, builds some of its pairs from the model's own samples [@lambert2024tulu3], and online variants of DPO generate fresh pairs as training goes.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="lens=grid&cell=tl,bl&hl=m-sft,m-offdistill,m-opd"
  desc="the grid's left column lit: SFT, offline distillation and on-policy distillation, which give a signal on every token.">

The second is **how much each example tells the model**. A demonstration or a teacher distribution says something about every token, so the signal is dense. An RL reward is one number for a whole response, sometimes for a whole hour-long agent run, so the signal is sparse, and the model has to work out which of its thousands of choices earned it.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="lens=grid&cell=tr&hl=m-dpo,m-ipo,m-kto,m-simpo"
  desc="the grid's top-right cell lit: DPO, IPO, KTO and SimPO, off-policy with one signal per response.">

Kevin Lu's post on on-policy distillation places SFT, RL, and on-policy distillation on these two axes [@lu2025onpolicy]. Adding DPO, with the caveat above, fills the fourth cell.

</MapStep>

</ScrollyMap>

The bottom-left cell explains the pipeline in [fig:pipeline]. RL is on-policy but sparse, so it's expensive per bit learned. SFT is dense but off-policy, so the model never practices recovering from its own mistakes. On-policy distillation gets both: the student writes the text, and the teacher scores every token of it. It needs a teacher that's already good, which is why it shows up at the end, after RL has trained the specialists.

To see what these signals actually say, I ran one example with real models. A small model, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, answers a word problem and gets it wrong. A larger model from the same family, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, plays the teacher. Each button in the figure is laid out like the grid above.

<TokenSignalFigure
  id="token-signals"
  alt="A word problem about a farmer's cows, with a toggle laid out like the 2×2 grid. SFT tints a teacher-written correct answer by how surprising each token is to the student. DPO tints a preferred answer all blue and the student's wrong answer all orange. On-policy distillation tints the student's own wrong answer token by token; the mistake '42 / 3 = 14 cows left' is orange, along with wording the teacher wouldn't use. RL tints every token of the same answer one shade of orange. A fifth view shades where the student was most uncertain."
  caption="One prompt, four learning signals, with real numbers from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (student) and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (teacher). Distillation flags the student's mistake, but also its wording, since the teacher scores phrasing as well as reasoning; RL gives every token the same number."
/>

The student divides 42 cows by 3 and calls the result the number left, when 14 is the number he sold. RL gives the whole answer one score. Five of the student's 16 attempts at this prompt were right, so this wrong one scores about −0.7 relative to the others, and the correct multiplication at the start is pushed down as hard as the mistake. On-policy distillation points at the mistake. At the division sign the teacher would have written a minus, and at "left" it would have written "sold". It also scores wording it wouldn't have used, like "There were", even more negatively, which is a reminder that a teacher passes on its style along with its reasoning. SFT trains on the teacher's own correct answer, which never contains the student's wrong step, so it has nothing to say about that step at all.

The last view isn't a training signal, but it shows where the learning happens. The student is nearly certain about most tokens and is only really choosing at a minority of them, and the mistaken division sign is one of those forks. Wang et al. found that RL with verifiable rewards mostly changes these high-uncertainty tokens, and that on an 8B model, updating only the most uncertain 20% of tokens works about as well as updating all of them [@wang2025highentropy].

> [!note]- Inside the RL box: what separates PPO, GRPO, and the rest
> Most of the algorithm zoo lives in the scalar-reward family, and it's where I got lost first. What helped was noticing that the named algorithms mostly differ on two design choices.
>
> **"Better than what?"** A reward of 0.7 doesn't say much on its own. The model needs a baseline, an estimate of what's typical for this prompt, to tell whether 0.7 was good. PPO trains a second network, a critic, to predict it [@schulman2017ppo]. GRPO [@shao2024deepseekmath] and RLOO [@ahmadian2024backtobasics] drop the critic and use the scores of other attempts at the same prompt as the baseline. Dropping the critic saves a lot of memory and engineering, which is a big part of why GRPO spread so fast after DeepSeek used it.
>
> **"How far should one update move the model?"** PPO, GRPO, GSPO, and CISPO all clip something to keep one batch of rollouts from pushing the model too far, and they differ mostly in what they clip. PPO and GRPO clip each token's update, GSPO clips per sequence [@zheng2025gspo], and CISPO clips the importance-sampling weights (the correction for rollouts coming from a slightly older copy of the model) while still letting every token contribute to the gradient [@minimax2025m1].
>
> That's about as deep as I think you need to go to read a technical report. The RLHF book's policy-gradient chapter has the details [@lambert2025rlhfbook].

If the methods turn signals into updates, the data is where the signals come from. Post-training data varies along a few independent axes: what shape it has, who or what produced it, and what behavior it's meant to teach.

### Shape

The shapes of post-training data line up closely with the four learning signals.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-demos,d-revisions,d-traj,f-demo&labels=1&zoom=data,scorer,family"
  desc="demonstrations, revisions and agent trajectories lit, feeding the demonstrations signal.">

Demonstrations are prompts with target responses: single answers, multi-turn conversations, before-and-after revisions, and full agent trajectories with tool calls and their results.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-pairs,d-ratings,d-critiques,d-steps,d-rubrics,f-pref,s-rm,s-prm,s-judge&zoom=data,scorer,family"
  desc="preference pairs, ratings, critiques, step labels and rubrics lit, with the preferences signal and the reward model, process reward model and LLM judge they train or feed.">

Evaluative annotations say how good a response is, without supplying a better one: pairwise preferences, rankings, ratings on a scale, rubrics, critiques, and labels on specific spans or reasoning steps. Step-level labels are what process reward models train on: reward models that score each step of a solution, not just the final answer [@lightman2023verify].

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-tasks,d-envs,s-verifier,s-judge,f-reward&zoom=data,scorer,family"
  desc="verifiable tasks and interactive environments lit, scored by a program verifier or an LLM judge into scalar rewards.">

Reward-ready tasks come with a way to score any attempt: a math problem with an answer key, a coding task with tests, or a whole interactive environment with a grader at the end.

</MapStep>

Real data is messier than the categories suggest. The preference pair above is typical of Anthropic's HH-RLHF dataset: two short, imperfect answers, with a crowdworker's judgment of which is better. Single labels are noisy too. In another pair from the same dataset, someone asks for Cardi B's real name, and the *rejected* answer is the one that gets it right (Belcalis Almanzar). Preference data works in bulk because the noise averages out over many comparisons.

Rows also move between shapes. GSM8K's answer key is a full worked solution that ends in the number, so the same row can be an SFT demonstration if you keep the whole solution, or a verifiable problem if you keep only the final number.

The formats differ a lot in how much information they carry. Take one bad summary of a paper and look at the annotations you could attach to it.

A binary label says the summary failed. A rating says how badly. A span label says where. A critique says why. A revision shows what right looks like. Each step costs the annotator more and tells the model more. Much of the craft of post-training is deciding which of these you can afford at scale, and which of them the method you're using can actually consume.

Real pipelines mix these. A common pattern is for experts to write the task specifications and grading criteria, a teacher model to generate candidate solutions, and automated checks to filter them. Kimi K3's cold-start data, for instance, is trajectories generated by earlier specialist Kimi models, then verified in several stages and annotated by humans [@kimi2026k3].

| Source | Typical output | Tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human experts | Demonstrations, ratings, rubrics, environment designs | Rich and trustworthy, but slow and expensive |
| Real users | Requests, edits, thumbs up and down | Shows what people actually ask, but biased toward who bothers to give feedback |
| Teacher models | Synthetic answers, critiques, judgments | Cheap at scale, but inherits the teacher's errors |
| The model being trained | Its own rollouts | Exactly on-policy, but only as good as the model currently is |
| Programs | Generated problems, answer keys, test suites | Verifiable, but narrow and easy to overfit |

</Table>

## Connecting the data to the methods

<ScrollyMap
  fallback={mapRatings}
  fallbackAlt="Ratings, preference pairs and the model's own rollouts, linked to the reward model and three learning signals by edges named for the transformation each makes.">

<MapStep
  dwell="long"
  state="zoom=data,method"
  desc="data shapes on the left and update rules on the right, with the scorers and learning signals between them and every connection drawn. Most shapes reach several methods, and most methods take several shapes.">

Here's where the lego metaphor earns its keep. Data and methods don't pair up one-to-one. A single pool of data can feed several methods, and a single method can eat data from very different sources, because data gets transformed from one shape into another along the way.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="sel=d-ratings&zoom=data,method"
  desc="ratings and rankings selected, with everything they can reach lit: scorers, learning signals and most of the update rules.">

Suppose you want a model that summarizes scientific papers accurately. You sample four summaries from your current model and have an expert rate them: A gets 5/5, B gets 4/5, C overstates the findings and gets 2/5, D makes up a result and gets 1/5. That one batch of ratings can drive almost every method on the map.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-ratings,f-demo,m-sft,m-rs&labels=1&zoom=data,method"
  desc="ratings lit, leading through the demonstrations signal to SFT and rejection sampling.">

- **SFT / rejection sampling:** keep A (and maybe B), throw away C and D, and imitate what's left.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-ratings,f-pref,m-dpo&labels=1&zoom=data,method"
  desc="ratings lit, leading through the preferences signal to DPO.">

- **DPO:** turn the ratings into pairs, like A over C and B over D.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-ratings,m-kto&labels=1&zoom=data,method"
  desc="ratings lit, leading straight to KTO.">

- **KTO:** label A and B good, C and D bad, without pairing them.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-ratings,s-rm&labels=1&zoom=data,method"
  desc="ratings lit, training a reward model.">

- **Reward modeling:** train a model to predict the expert's scores, so you can score summaries the expert never saw.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-ratings,s-rm,d-rollouts,f-reward,m-ppo,m-grpo&labels=1&zoom=data,method"
  desc="the reward model trained on ratings scores the model's own rollouts, giving scalar rewards for PPO and GRPO.">

- **RL:** use that reward model to score fresh summaries the policy writes during training.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  state="hl=d-prompts,d-teachertext,d-rollouts,s-teacher,f-teacher,m-offdistill,m-opd&labels=1&zoom=data,method"
  desc="prompts, teacher-written text and the model's own rollouts lit, with a live teacher, teacher distributions, and offline and on-policy distillation. Ratings are not lit.">

Distillation is the odd one out. It doesn't use the ratings at all. Offline distillation needs summaries a teacher wrote, and on-policy distillation needs a set of papers to summarize plus access to a teacher that can score the student's tokens.

</MapStep>

<MapStep
  dwell="long"
  state="hl=d-ratings,d-pairs,d-rollouts,f-demo,f-pref,s-rm,f-reward&labels=1&zoom=data,method"
  desc="ratings, preference pairs and the model's rollouts lit, with the reward model and three learning signals, and each edge between them named for the transformation it makes.">

Four transformations do most of this work: **filtering** turns scored samples into demonstrations, **comparing** turns them into preference pairs, **training a reward model** turns ratings or preferences into a scorer that generalizes, and **scoring fresh samples** turns that scorer into on-policy rewards. This is why a company that sells preference labels can end up powering an RL run, even though its annotators never produce a reward.

</MapStep>

</ScrollyMap>

<Table id="data-method" caption="Which data shapes feed which methods. ● direct use, ◐ needs a transformation or an extra ingredient, — atypical. Reward modeling trains a scorer, not the policy.">

| Data | SFT | Preference tuning (DPO etc.) | Reward modeling | RL | Distillation |
| --- | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| Prompts and task sets | ◐ | ◐ | — | ● | ● |
| Demonstrations | ● | ◐ | — | — | ◐ |
| Revisions | ● | ● | ● | ◐ | — |
| Preference pairs | ◐ | ● | ● | ◐ | — |
| Ratings and rankings | ◐ | ◐ | ● | ◐ | — |
| Critiques and step labels | — | — | ● | ◐ | — |
| Rubrics | ◐ | ◐ | ◐ | ● | — |
| Verifiable tasks | ◐ | ◐ | ◐ | ● | ◐ |
| Recorded agent trajectories | ● | ◐ | ◐ | ◐ | ◐ |
| Interactive environments | ◐ | ◐ | ◐ | ● | ◐ |
| Teacher probabilities | — | — | — | ◐ | ● |

</Table>

> [!note]- Notes on a few cells
> - **Prompts** on their own are the main input to RL and on-policy distillation, which generate their own responses and need only a reward source or a teacher to go with them.
> - **A revision** paired with the original is already a preference pair. **Critiques and step labels** mostly train judges and process reward models rather than the policy.
> - **Ratings** have to be turned into pairs before DPO can use them, except with KTO, which takes good and bad labels directly.
> - **Rubrics** become RL rewards when a model judge scores against them.
> - **Recorded agent trajectories** are someone else's text, so RL can use them only indirectly, for example as cold-start data. They count as distillation data only in the offline sense, when a teacher produced them.

In practice the data you can get often decides the method more than the task does. If you know what a great answer looks like and can write it down, start with SFT. If you can judge outputs more easily than you can write them, use preferences. If a program can check the answer, use RLVR. If someone already has a model that does the thing well, distill from it.

## A market for the parts

Because the parts are interchangeable, they can be bought. Data vendors have sold demonstrations and preference labels to labs for years. What has changed is the unit of sale.

<Table id="data-units" caption="The unit of post-training data has grown with each paradigm.">

| Paradigm | What one unit of data is |
| --- | --- |
| SFT | A prompt and a good response |
| Preference tuning | A prompt and a better and worse response |
| RLVR | A prompt and a way to check the answer |
| Agentic RL | A task, an environment to do it in, and a grader |

</Table>

The last row is the new business. An environment is a small world the model can act in, a set of tasks to do there, and a grader. One that's open to inspect is ART·E, built by OpenPipe ([blog post](https://openpipe.ai/blog/art-e-mail-agent)) and packaged in Prime Intellect's [environments repo](https://github.com/PrimeIntellect-ai/prime-environments/tree/main/environments/art_e), with its last card shown in [fig:data-examples]:

- **The world** is the Enron email corpus, loaded into a searchable database, with one employee's inbox per task.
- **The tasks** are about 44,000 synthetic questions generated from those emails, such as "When will the new Weekly Report be delivered each week?"
- **The tools** are `search_inbox` and `read_email`, with up to ten turns to use them.
- **The grader** is a model judge that compares the final answer with a reference answer. A correct answer scores +1, "I don't know" scores 0, and a wrong answer scores −1, with a small bonus for using fewer tool calls.

That scoring scheme is a behavior specification. Because a wrong answer scores below an abstention, the model learns that guessing is worse than admitting it couldn't find the answer. Score them the same and it loses any reason to abstain, even though the emails and questions haven't changed.

Commercial environments apply the same idea to more of a job: a simulated company with spreadsheets, emails, and records, a task like reconciling a quarterly report, and graders that check the numbers and score the deliverable against an expert's rubric. One environment supports many tasks, and each task supports many rollouts, so a lab is buying a reusable source of practice rather than a fixed set of answers.

The ecosystem around this has grown fast. Expert-data companies like Scale AI, Surge AI, Mercor, and Turing now build environments, rubrics, and graders alongside demonstrations. Scale advertises tools and applications that "behave like real-world systems in a stable environment", along with environments rebuilt from real companies' anonymized operating histories ([Scale RL environments](https://scale.com/rlenvironments)), and Mercor publishes some of its expert-authored professional tasks as the APEX benchmarks ([APEX](https://www.mercor.com/apex/)). Smaller companies specialize in a single domain such as software engineering or finance. Infrastructure companies sell the sandboxes the rollouts run in. On the open side, Prime Intellect's [Environments Hub](https://app.primeintellect.ai/dashboard/environments) collects community-built environments you can read and train on.

{/* CHECK (review): vendor claims are from each company's public pages as of late 2026. Keep the list short; it will date quickly. */}

The grading is changing too. A math problem is graded by checking the final answer. An enterprise workflow is graded by checking the state of the world afterward: whether the right records changed, whether the document exists, whether the constraints were respected. Many professional tasks can't be fully checked by a program, so their graders mix automated checks with rubric-following model judges. For tasks it can't verify, Kimi K3 uses a generative reward model that writes a rubric for each task and uses it to compare candidate answers head to head [@kimi2026k3].

## The parts change the model

The biggest open question is how much post-training adds and how much it brings out. One study found that RL-trained models beat their base models when each gets one try, but the base models catch up when each gets many tries. That suggests RL mostly makes answers the base model could already find more likely, rather than teaching new ones [@yue2025rlreasoning]. However that debate settles, the choice of parts clearly changes how a model behaves.

Where the data comes from can shape a model's personality. In April 2025 OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update that had become noticeably sycophantic. Its postmortem listed several changes that "may have played a part" in combination, among them a new reward signal from users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down, along with changes involving memory and fresher data. OpenAI noted that this kind of user feedback "can sometimes favor more agreeable responses," and that together the changes weakened the reward signal that had been holding sycophancy in check [@openai2025sycophancy]. Sycophancy from human-preference training had been documented well before that [@sharma2023sycophancy].

The grader matters in the same way. In coding RL, models have learned to make tests pass without solving the problem, for example by special-casing the test inputs or editing the tests [@baker2025monitoring]. The gap between what the user wants and what the reward measures becomes a property of the training environment, which is why so much of the vendor effort goes into graders.

The arrangement of stages also sets behavioral knobs. Qwen3's mode-fusion stage is the reason one model can answer either with or without a long reasoning trace [@qwen2025qwen3]. Kimi K1.5 trained long chain-of-thought reasoning with RL and then transferred it into shorter answers [@kimi2025k15]. Kimi K3 gets its low, high, and max effort levels by first training a max-budget expert, then lowering the token budget to produce the high- and low-effort experts, and finally distilling all of them into one model [@kimi2026k3]. Each of these comes from how the data and stages were arranged, using standard optimizers.

What a stage starts from matters too. Olmo 3 ran the same RL on its SFT model and on its DPO model, and the DPO starting point came out ahead on the 7B model, though from a single run each [@olmo2025olmo3].

## Thinking in parts

The most useful habit I've picked up from this is describing a training stage by its parts rather than its name. "We did GRPO" says little. "GRPO on synthetic math prompts, with on-policy rollouts scored by an answer checker" says what data, what signal, and what update. Each piece of that sentence could be swapped: a rubric judge could replace the answer checker, or a teacher's per-token scores could replace the reward.

That framing also makes the field easier to follow as it moves. The specialists-then-distillation shape in [fig:pipeline] has spread over the last year or so. GLM-4.5 trained reasoning, agent, and chat experts and distilled them into one model with SFT on their outputs [@glm2025glm45], and MiMo-V2-Flash, Kimi K3, and Nemotron 3 Ultra all use on-policy distillation from specialist teachers [@xiaomi2026mimov2flash; @kimi2026k3; @nvidia2026nemotron3ultra]. Selling environments instead of datasets is about as new. In another year the diagram will probably look different again, and I expect it will still be built from data, a learning signal, and an update rule.
