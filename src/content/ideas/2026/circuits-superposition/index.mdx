## Toy Models of Superposition

### Core claim

Small ReLU networks trained to reconstruct synthetic features demonstrate that a model can represent more features than it has dimensions, provided the features are sparse enough — a phenomenon the paper calls **superposition**. Because the toy setup has known ground-truth features, the origins and dynamics of polysemanticity can be fully understood in it: polysemantic neurons are what superposition looks like from the perspective of a single neuron.

  caption={"The superposition hypothesis: the network we observe is a low-dimensional projection of a larger network in which every neuron is a disentangled feature. From the perspective of individual neurons in the observed model, this presents as polysemanticity. Figure from the paper."}
/>

### Key results

In the toy setting the paper demonstrates superposition directly: models really do store more features than they have dimensions, and both monosemantic and polysemantic neurons form along the way. Some computation can happen entirely in superposition (the toy models compute absolute value on superposed features, not just store them). Whether a given feature gets a dedicated dimension, shares capacity in superposition, or is dropped entirely is governed by a phase change in feature sparsity and importance. And superposed features arrange themselves into regular geometric structures — antipodal pairs, triangles, pentagons, tetrahedra.

<Figure
  src={sparsityGeometry}
  alt={"Feature embeddings in a 2D toy model at 0%, 80%, and 90% sparsity: dedicated orthogonal dimensions, antipodal pairs, then a pentagon with interference"}
  caption={"As feature sparsity increases, a 2-dimensional model goes from representing only the two most important features orthogonally, to packing four features as antipodal pairs, to embedding all five as a pentagon — at the cost of interference between them. Figure from the paper."}
/>

## Superposition, Memorization, and Double Descent

The follow-up extends the same toy models to ask what overfitting looks like mechanically. In these models, memorization turns out to be superposition of a different thing: trained on small datasets, the model stores individual training examples in superposition; trained on large datasets, it stores features. The two regimes are visibly different in the learned geometry, and the test-loss peak of double descent lands exactly in the transition between them.

<Figure
  src={ddRegimes}

## What the evidence shows

{/* NOTE: seeded evaluative draft — check it against your own read and rewrite in your voice before publishing */}

- Everything here is demonstrated in toy models where the ground-truth features are known by construction. That is the point (full understanding is available), but it means the transfer to real language models is, at this stage of the thread, a hypothesis.
- The practical consequence motivates the rest of the thread: if real models use superposition, then analyzing individual neurons cannot recover the features, no matter how careful the analysis. Something like dictionary learning is required, which is exactly where [Towards Monosemanticity](/ideas/2026/circuits-monosemanticity/) picks up.
- The double-descent result connects superposition to generalization, but in an even more restricted setting; it is suggestive rather than central to the superposition story.

## Since the paper

The toy-model program keeps running inside the thread: a 2025 note unpacks the ["interference weights"](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/interference-weights/index.html) that superposition forces on a model, and the [July 2025 circuits update](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/july-update/index.html) reworks the original framework paper's ideas around extracting features from superposition. The larger legacy is indirect: superposition is the argument for the entire dictionary-learning line — sparse autoencoders exist because this paper argued neurons can't be the unit of analysis. How well real-model features match the toy picture is still being tested; the [monosemanticity notes](/ideas/2026/circuits-monosemanticity/) cover how that has gone.
